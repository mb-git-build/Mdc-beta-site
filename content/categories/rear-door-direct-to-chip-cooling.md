# Rear-Door & Direct-to-Chip Cooling

Rear-door heat exchangers and direct-to-chip cooling often appear in the same executive conversation because both offer a path beyond conventional air cooling without fully committing to immersion. But they are not interchangeable. This category exists to help buyers understand where rack-adjacent cooling, chip-level liquid capture, and surrounding loop design fit inside a serious high-density compute deployment.

This is one of the most important bridge categories in MDC because it connects thermal architecture directly to rack power, monitoring, retrofit feasibility, and operational change.

## Why this category matters

- many buyers reach this category when air cooling is no longer enough, but immersion still feels too operationally disruptive
- the choice between rear-door and direct-to-chip shapes facility integration, service workflow, leak tolerance, density assumptions, and long-term upgrade paths
- this category reveals whether a project is solving a thermal bottleneck pragmatically or just chasing the newest cooling narrative
- it is often the most realistic path for dense retrofits and phased AI capacity expansion

## Where it fits in a dense compute deployment

- sits between conventional room cooling and deeper liquid or immersion architectures
- frequently depends on liquid-loop systems, monitoring layers, rack-power design, and upstream thermal rejection capacity
- becomes central in retrofits, high-density colo expansions, and staged AI rollout programs where service continuity matters
- often serves as the bridge between executive urgency and facility-team risk tolerance

## What to compare

- rear-door heat exchangers versus direct-to-chip based on density, service model, and retrofit complexity
- loop requirements, CDU strategy, and how the cooling path connects to plant-level rejection
- operational visibility across leak detection, telemetry, alerting, and thermal monitoring
- effect on rack layout, power distribution, cable routing, and maintenance access
- whether the vendor ecosystem includes enough integration and operations support to move beyond pilot conditions

## Typical buyer questions

- Is rear-door enough for our target density, or are we already in direct-to-chip territory?
- How disruptive is each path to service workflows, technician access, and day-two operations?
- What extra categories become critical once we choose one of these architectures?
- Can the existing plant absorb the heat if rack-level capture improves?
- Which deployment path is most believable for a fast retrofit versus a new AI hall?

## Decision scenarios

Rear-door and direct-to-chip should be compared as operating models, not just hardware options.

- Retrofit density upgrade: assess rear-door first if the goal is faster deployment with less change to server internals, then compare against direct-to-chip if the density curve keeps rising.
- New AI hall: evaluate direct-to-chip where power density, coolant loop design, and long-term scale justify deeper integration.
- Colocation AI expansion: test whether the operator truly supports rack-adjacent or chip-level liquid paths in production rather than only in marketing language.
- Phased cooling strategy: use this category to understand whether rear-door is a durable endpoint or only an intermediate step toward broader liquid architecture.

## Deployment signals

- the project needs more density than standard air cooling can support comfortably
- the team wants a path between conventional cooling and full immersion
- retrofit realism, serviceability, and operational familiarity matter as much as peak density
- buyers are now asking how loop design, rack power, monitoring, and mechanical rejection interact
